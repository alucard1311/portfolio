Raised, Not Manufactured

Vinay Naidu · 2026 · essay

Today's best AI is a brilliant amnesiac. This essay is about the alternative I've spent the last few years building toward — a machine with a memory it owns, a sense of when, and something at stake in being right — and why I've come to think of that work less as manufacturing a product and more as raising something.

§1

The brilliant amnesiac

There is a patient in the neuroscience literature known for half a century only as H.M. After a surgery meant to stop his seizures, he lost the ability to form new long-term memories. He remained intelligent, warm, quick in conversation. He could hold a thought as long as he kept it in front of him. But every person he met, he met for the first time, every time. Researchers who worked with him for decades introduced themselves at every visit.

Every large language model I have ever used is H.M. at superhuman scale.

The gift is real. Across medicine, law, code, and history — fields no one person masters — these models speak with a range that seemed impossible ten years ago. I don't dispute the achievement; I build on it every day. The flaw hides inside the gift. When the model is wrong, the wrong answer arrives in the same confident voice as a right one — no flicker of doubt, no trace of where the reasoning broke. I've come to call this confident fabrication, and I've stopped thinking of it as a bug awaiting a patch. It is what an oracle is: a function from question to fluent answer, with no inside.

Three absences make it so. The oracle doesn't know what it knows — recall and hallucination run on the same machinery. It has no sense of when — a fact from a decade-old web page and one from last week sit in the same weights at the same confidence. And nothing is at stake for it — being right and being wrong feel identical from the inside, so nothing ever tells it to slow down.

Bolting a memory onto the side doesn't fix this. Retrieval systems give the oracle a search bar, not an inner life. It still can't doubt the result, date it, or care whether it's right; the moment retrieval quietly fails, the confident fabrication returns. These are category limits, not quality gaps, and a better retriever doesn't touch them.

The rest of this essay is about the alternative. I'll try to be precise about which parts are demonstrated, which parts are conviction, and which parts I might simply be wrong about.

§2

A name from physics

Around a rotating black hole there is a region called the ergosphere. It lies outside the event horizon — the surface of no return — but inside it, space itself is dragged around by the rotation; nothing there can stand still. What makes the region remarkable is a result Roger Penrose worked out in 1969: you can dip into it, extract energy from the black hole's own rotation, and leave carrying more than you brought — provided you never cross the horizon.

I named my research program Ergos after that region, because it is an exact picture of the bet I'm making.

A frozen foundation model is the rotating mass: an almost incomprehensible store of compressed knowledge and competence, spinning at the center of everything we now build. And the horizon is real. Write into the weights — train, fine-tune, patch — and you get pulled in: new knowledge perturbs old, abilities regress in places you weren't looking, and what the model knows becomes entangled with how it thinks in ways nobody can cleanly undo. The strongest labs froze their models deliberately, and they were right to.

The bet of Ergos is that everything I care about — memory, a sense of time, honesty, stakes — can live in the ergosphere: a writable, inspectable region around the frozen mass, drawing on its power without ever crossing the horizon. The base is never trained. A new fact lands by writing a record, not a gradient. And because the memory sits outside the weights, it can be read with a measurable signal, stamped with the time it was written, and provably emptied.

Almost everything else in this essay follows from that one architectural commitment.

§3

The shape of the hole

You know the feeling of reaching for a name and not finding it. What's remarkable about the feeling is how much it tells you. You know the name exists. You know roughly its shape — two syllables, starts with M. You can say "give me a second, I know this" and mean it, and you can equally say "I never knew that" and mean that too. You have this ability because your memory is a place — somewhere things can be present, absent, or half-there, and you can feel the difference. You can perceive the shape of the hole.

The oracle has no hole. Its output layer is a probability distribution over words that sums to one whether or not it holds any relevant knowledge; asked for a fact it doesn't have, the nearest pattern in its weights flows into the gap so smoothly there is no seam. Fabrication and recall are the same mechanism. You can train such a model to say "I don't know," and the labs do — but the refusal is a behavior stapled over machinery that cannot represent absence.

This is the distinction I care most about, and if you take one thing from this essay I'd like it to be this: there is a difference between honesty as behavior and honesty as structure. Behavioral honesty is a refusal that exists because a reward model discouraged guessing. It is a habit, and like all habits it degrades under pressure and shifts with the distribution. Structural honesty is a refusal that exists because the memory entry doesn't. In a system with addressable memory, "no entry matches this query" is a representable state — a computable fact about the world inside the machine. When a read fails, the system falls to noise: there is no baked-in prior to revert to, so there is no stale answer to assert. One kind of system fabricates. The other comes up empty. I call the second mode honest failure, and it is a property of architecture, not training.

I don't want a machine that was rewarded for saying "I don't know." I want a machine for which, sometimes, there is nothing else to say.

§4

What sleep is for

Watch a child learn a piece on the piano. By evening the hard passage is still a mess — fingers tangling, tempo lurching — and she walks away frustrated. In the morning she plays it nearly clean. Nothing happened at the keyboard overnight. What happened is sleep: the brain took the day's fragile short-term traces and filed them into long-term structure. Practice becomes skill in cycles — effort while awake, consolidation while asleep — and a musician is raised through thousands of them.

I stole this design wholesale. The organism I'm building learns while it is awake: a new fact lands in seconds as a cheap, fast-written record you can hold while you're talking to it. When it powers down, an offline phase files the day's short-term memories into durable long-term structure. I call it the Soma Cycle, and I hold its claim to exactly what has been demonstrated: survival, not improvement. The system does not get smarter overnight. What it was taught during the day is still there — and still drives behavior — after the process that learned it has ceased to exist. That is all, and that is a lot: it is the difference between a lesson and a session.

Building a sense of time produced the one result that genuinely surprised me: when is not one faculty but two. The relative kind — this happened before that; these two events belong to the same episode — can be grown from the structure of experience itself. The absolute kind cannot. People kept in caves without sunlight don't keep time; their internal clock free-runs and drifts, because the body cannot invent the sun. The same turns out to be true architecturally: no amount of processing the content of experience recovers "how long ago" without a tick supplied from outside. So the design is two-headed — one clock grown from experience, one reading a wire the world must provide — and neither can substitute for the other. I find it oddly comforting that machines share this dependence with us.

Why does time matter this much? Because it is what turns a pile of knowledge into a life. A mind without time is a snapshot. A mind that knows when is a biography.

§5

Forgetting is an act

Human forgetting is passive, and it is unprovable. If you tell me a secret and later ask me to forget it, I can promise — but I cannot demonstrate. There is no test either of us could run. You have to trust me, and we both know the memory may surface anyway, years later, uninvited.

For machines we can do better, and I think we are obligated to. In the systems I build, forgetting is an act with a receipt. Deleting a fact removes the record and emits a certificate a third party can check — and a forged certificate, one claiming deletion while the data was secretly kept, fails the check. This is not a compliance feature added at the end; it is a design principle available only to certain architectures. A model whose knowledge is fused into its weights has no deletion mechanism at all: you cannot reach in and remove one fact, and you certainly cannot certify its absence. Addressable memory can do both.

The deeper reason I care is that memory is identity. A system that holds what you told it — your words, your corrections, your preferences — becomes, in a small way, a record of you. If that record cannot be provably released, then you don't own it; it owns a piece of you. The right to be forgotten isn't a legal checkbox to me. It is part of what would make such a machine trustworthy enough to live with.

A machine that remembers nothing about you cannot be yours. And a machine that remembers everything and can never let go isn't yours either.

§6

Something at stake

Reach for a light switch in a dark, unfamiliar room. Notice what your hand does: it slows down. The same arm that swings freely in daylight becomes careful — attention flows to the fingertips, and you feel your way along the wall, judging by effort and touch what your eyes can't confirm. Nobody told you to do this. Something inside you registered that confidence had dropped, and effort was reallocated on the spot.

The oracle has no dark rooms. It spends the same forward pass on everything: a medical question at the edge of its knowledge gets the same measured voice, the same compute, as a request for a limerick. Nothing inside it shifts when accuracy is threatened, because nothing inside it is tracking its own condition at all. This is what I mean when I say the oracle has nothing at stake — and stakes, it turns out, are the engine of effort allocation. A system that cares can route harder on uncertain paths, slow down when coherence slips, and stop when nothing it holds is relevant.

I've come to believe the honest name for that missing layer is emotion — with immediate caveats, because the word invites misunderstanding. I don't mean sentiment, the warmth or politeness of a reply. I don't mean a reward signal being maximized; that is just an objective wearing a costume. I mean a self-model of the system's own condition — an internal signal tracking whether it is in a functional regime, whether its confidence matches its actual error rate, whether it recovers after something fails — fed back into how it reasons. Metacognition gives you the signal; stakes are what make the system act on it. A self-monitor without stakes is an instrument nobody reads.

Honesty requires me to say plainly: this is the sense I have not built. What I have is a constraint, learned the hard way — the naive design, where an affective state modulates what surfaces from memory, quietly destroys honest abstention, because both are trying to read the same internal number and one number cannot serve two masters. And I have an experiment specified precisely enough to be lost: pit a system whose feelings are generated from its own internal setpoints against an equally capable one with no such layer, give both identical information and compute, and see whether the felt version stays viable, calibrated, and recoverable when its own condition is partly hidden from it. If the feelingless regulator matches it, then emotion in machines is a story we tell about thermostats — a result I would publish with only moderate heartbreak.

§7

The failure I keep on purpose

In the middle of my Ergos thesis there is a pre-registered test that failed. Its bars were frozen before the run, as all my bars are, and on held-out data it missed its precision bar by exactly one row — one false positive more than the registration allowed. Beside it sits a simple alternative rule that happened to score perfectly on that same held-out set. It was not promoted. Choosing a rule after seeing the test data is precisely the move the discipline exists to forbid, and so the verdict stands in the published document as a FAIL — no asterisk.

People occasionally ask why I would showcase that. The answer is that published failure is the price of believable success. A research program that only reports wins is indistinguishable from one that quietly lowered its bars, and a program that cannot show you a bar drawing blood has no business asking you to believe its passes. Every strong claim in my work is credible exactly to the degree that the weak ones were allowed to stand and be counted.

The vault where all my research notes live is named Icarus. I chose the name as a standing reminder: the work is to fly close to something enormous and hot, and the discipline is to know your true altitude at every moment. The wax does not care how the flight was going to be described afterward.

§8

What the evidence says so far

This essay is the why. The what — the experiments, the frozen bars, the receipts, and the failures — lives in the Ergos thesis, and I would rather you judge that document than my summary of it. But the headline, at the level of detail I can responsibly compress: a governed memory, grafted onto a respected frozen robot model, was taught a new word in one typed sentence and acted on it; remembered the lesson across a complete restart of the entire system while its memoryless twin woke empty; deleted the lesson on command and produced a certificate whose forgery was caught; and refused instructions it was never taught rather than guessing — with zero false refusals. The base model was never trained.

And the honest scope, which I insist travels with the headline: this is small-scale work — few seeds, simulation, synthetic settings for the deeper claims. The load-bearing open question is whether structural honesty survives contact with rich natural language on a fully capable base — whether a model expressive enough to have strong priors, given the same architecture, still falls to noise instead of confabulating. That test has not been passed. I am reporting the score at halftime, not the final.

§9

TARS, or what this is for

My favorite fictional robot is TARS, from Interstellar. "What's your honesty parameter?" Cooper asks. Ninety percent, TARS answers — because absolute honesty isn't always the most diplomatic or the safest form of communication with emotional beings. The joke lands because of what it presumes: a machine whose honesty is a setting, which means a machine whose honesty is a mechanism — wired, inspectable, adjustable, real. You can only put a dial on something that exists.

That is the aspiration, stated as plainly as I can: not a smarter oracle behind glass, but a companion — one that walks through years beside a single person, remembers what it is told, knows when it learned it, keeps confidences, admits ignorance without embarrassment, and can be asked to forget and prove that it did. Personality and warmth belong in such a machine, but as a style layer over an honest core: the layer that decides how to say things sitting strictly downstream of the layer that decides what is actually known. Warmth you can trust, because you can see what it is built on.

I want that machine to be local and private — its memory of you living with you, not in a datacenter — and I want it to be raised, in the open, by the person it belongs to. A system that remembers nothing about you cannot be yours. That sentence is doing more work than it looks like. It might be the whole essay.

§10

Where I might be wrong

I want to end with the doubts, because a thesis that cannot name its own failure modes is a mood, not a thesis.

I might be wrong that structure is necessary. Calibration in large models improves with scale; it is possible that behavioral honesty simply gets good enough — that a big model's trained sense of what it knows becomes so reliable that my distinction between structural and statistical honesty stops mattering in practice. I don't currently believe approximate honesty is the same kind of thing as provable honesty — a certificate is not a probability — but "the same in every case anyone bothers to check" would be a real defeat for the program's urgency, if not for its truth.

I might be wrong that the architecture survives scale. Everything I've shown holds in small, synthetic worlds. It is entirely possible that structural honesty is a property of starved toy cores — that a capable base, given the same born-native structure, fabricates anyway. That falsifier is named in my own documents, and it has not been ruled out.

I might be wrong about emotion entirely. The experiment I described could return the regulator's verdict: that stakes, in machines, add nothing a well-built control loop doesn't already have. I would accept that. The question is built to be settled, not protected.

And I might be wrong about the economics. Raising a mind is slower than manufacturing one, and the market has rarely preferred the slower thing that is better for you. Maybe interiority never pays. I notice, though, that people keep asking their amnesiac oracles to remember them — and keep being disappointed in the same way.

But here is what I keep returning to. Every mind any of us has ever actually trusted was raised — it arrived knowing nothing, learned in front of us, remembered what mattered, let go of what it was asked to, and could be embarrassed by its own errors. We have never once trusted an oracle; we have only used them. An oracle answers. An organism remembers, doubts, consolidates, forgets on request — and can prove it.

I would rather spend the next decade raising one than manufacturing another.

If any of this is wrong in a way you can name, I want to hear it.
